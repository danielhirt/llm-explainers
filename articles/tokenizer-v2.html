The token economy

A language model never sees words. It sees integer IDs from a learned vocabulary, and every one of those IDs is a line item. This page trains a real tokenizer in your browser, shows you where it sits inside the machine, and puts a price on everything it cuts.

Section 0 · The machine

Where the tokenizer sits

Every chat model runs the same loop. Your text goes in, a tokenizer cuts it into tokens, and each token becomes an integer ID. Embeddings turn the IDs into vectors, a transformer reads all of them at once, and out comes a probability for every token in the vocabulary. A sampler picks one. That token joins the context, and the whole machine runs again to pick the next one.

Watch one full trip below. Two things to hold on to. The tokenizer is the front door: every stage after it works on tokens, never on text. And the loop meters everything twice, because the model reads input tokens on every pass and emits output tokens one at a time. Both meters are what the invoice counts, which is why this whole page keeps score in tokens and dollars.

Each figure on this page is one station of that map. The pin in every figure header shows you where you are standing.

Section 1 · The splitter

The cut you are billed for

The unit of exchange is the token: a chunk of text that maps to one integer ID in a fixed vocabulary. A token is usually a word, part of a word, or a piece of punctuation. Whole words would need a vocabulary with millions of entries and would still miss new words. Single characters keep the vocabulary tiny but stretch every sentence into a long, expensive sequence. Tokens sit in between: common strings get their own entry, and rare strings break into smaller pieces.

Type below and watch the cut. Each chip is one token; ␣ marks a leading space, which belongs to the token. Click any chip to see its ID and how the tokenizer assembled it.

The stats under the splitter now come with a price meter. Context windows are measured in tokens, rate limits are measured in tokens, and the invoice at the end of the month is measured in tokens. One request is noise, so the meter also scales your text to a million requests, which is where token counts turn into budget lines.

Notice two things. Common English words from the corpus come out as single tokens. Anything the tokenizer never saw during training, like German, digits, or code, shatters into small pieces. The same amount of text then costs more tokens, and the meter shows what that does to money. That is not a bug. The cut shows you what the training data looked like, and the bill inherits it.

Section 2 · The vocabulary

Where the vocabulary comes from

Nobody sits down and writes a token list. An algorithm called byte-pair encoding learns the vocabulary from data. The whole algorithm fits in one sentence: start with single characters, count every adjacent pair in the corpus, glue the most frequent pair into a new token, and repeat. The ordered list of glue operations, called merges, is the tokenizer. To split new text later, the tokenizer replays the same merges in the same order.

The machine below runs byte-pair encoding for real on a small corpus written for this page. The forge on top shows each round: the three most frequent pairs line up, the winner fuses into a new token, and the new token drops into the vocabulary wall. Step through it and watch character soup turn into words. Turn on quiz mode to guess which pair merges next before the machine does.

Every tokenizer you ever used, including the one in front of your favorite chat model, came from exactly this process. The only difference is scale: terabytes of text and a few hundred thousand merges instead of 3 KB and a couple hundred. The consequences are identical: whatever was common in the training data becomes cheap, and everything else becomes expensive. Your invoice inherits the training distribution.

Halftime · The handoff

You built the input format. Now meet the machine that eats it.

Everything above happens before the model does any thinking, and everything below happens after. The tokenizer hands over a sequence of IDs, and from there the job is one question asked over and over: given the tokens so far, which token comes next? The next three figures replace the real transformer with something deliberately primitive, a table of counts, so you can see the shape of the job without billions of weights in the way. The interface is identical: context in, distribution out, one token appended, around again.

Section 4 · The predictor

Predicting the next token

The answer to the question is never a single token. It is a probability for every token in the vocabulary, and something downstream picks one. This page's predictor counts which token followed which pair of tokens in the corpus, and turns the counts into probabilities. A real model replaces the counting with a transformer that holds billions of learned weights and reads the whole context window, not just the last two tokens. The shape of the interface is identical, and that is the point: context in, distribution out.

Section 5 · The dice

Temperature and the dice

The distribution is not the output. Something has to pick one token from it, and how you pick changes everything about how the text feels. Greedy decoding always takes the single most likely token. It is safe and repetitive; run it long enough and it walks into loops. Sampling proportionally to the probabilities adds variety and risk. Temperature reshapes the distribution before the draw, and top-k throws away all but the k most likely tokens. Try the extremes and watch the same machine turn from a stuck record into word salad.

The dice are also where the second meter starts running. Every token the sampler emits is an output token, and output list prices run about five times input. A model that rambles for 400 tokens where 80 would do is not just slower to read. It is five times the money on the expensive meter, once per request, forever. Verbosity is a cost knob, and this figure is the knob's mechanism.

Section 6 · The bill machine

Your whole bill, three levers

Put the two meters together and a monthly bill is four numbers multiplied: tokens in, tokens out, requests, and price per token. The machine below is the entire model reduced to the arithmetic a finance team sees. The defaults describe a modest internal assistant: a 1,500 token system prompt, 2,000 tokens of history and retrieval per request, a 400 token answer, 100,000 requests a day.

Then pull the three levers engineers actually control. Trim the prompt: a token you delete is deleted from every request forever. Cache the stable prefix: providers re-read a prefix they have already seen for roughly a tenth of the input rate, and your system prompt is the same bytes every time. Batch what nobody waits for: overnight work runs at about half price. None of the three touch model quality. That is why cost reviews start with tokens, not with model choice.

The predictor fails in an instructive way: it has no idea what the sentence is about, only which token pairs were popular. When you beat it in Figure 5, you win by using context it cannot see. The step from counting to a transformer is precisely the step that closes that gap, and it is the subject of the deep dive: how token IDs become vectors, how attention lets every position read the others, and how training picks the weights. And when a bill surprises you, the explanation is almost always upstream in the cut: some text cost more tokens than anyone guessed, multiplied by every request. You now have the tools to check.
