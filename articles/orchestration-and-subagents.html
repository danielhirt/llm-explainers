Orchestration and subagents

One context window is the wrong shape for big work. The fix is delegation with a return contract.

An agent doing real work accumulates debris. Every file it reads, every search that comes back empty, every test log and dead end lands in the context window and stays there. It all stays because the context window is the model's only memory. On a large task the debris wins. A context meant for reasoning fills with raw material, and retrieval from the middle of it degrades. Eventually the harness must summarize the history to make room, which throws away detail at exactly the moment detail might matter.

A subagent is the escape hatch. Spawn a fresh model instance, a worker, with its own empty context window. Hand it one scoped job and take back a distilled result. The worker reads the 50 files and burns its own context. The parent, usually called the orchestrator, receives three sentences of conclusion. The parent's window carries the decisions and findings; the process that produced them disappears with the worker.

The economics

Delegation costs more tokens in total and less of everything that matters. Four workers reading 40,000 tokens each spend four times what one agent would spend to read the same material serially. They finish in roughly the wall-clock time of the slowest worker. Meanwhile the orchestrator's window, the scarce resource on a long-running task, stays almost empty. It holds the plan and four short results instead of 160,000 tokens of raw reading. You trade money for the two things one context cannot buy: parallel wall-clock time and headroom to keep reasoning at full quality.

The consequence is that a subagent's prompt carries unusual weight. A worker wakes up with no conversation history, no idea what you already tried, and no access to the reasoning that scoped its task. The prompt is its entire world. Orchestration failures trace back to underspecified delegation more often than to any model limitation. Say what the worker should return, in what form, and what it should not do. Otherwise, plan to pay for the round trip twice.

The patterns

A handful of shapes cover nearly everything people build with subagents.

Fan-out

Independent shards of the same job run in parallel: one worker per module in an audit, or per search angle in a research sweep. Fan-out is the default and the least fragile, because workers never need to know about each other. The orchestrator's job is to pick shard boundaries that keep workers independent.

Pipeline

Each item flows through stages: find, then fix, then check, with no barrier between them. Item A can sit in review while item B is still in the find stage. Pipelines beat staged batches on wall-clock time whenever stage durations vary, which is always. The orchestrator forces a barrier only when a stage needs every prior result at once. For example, one stage might deduplicate findings, and another might stop the run early when there is nothing to do.

Judge and jury

Verification runs as its own delegation. Each finding from the discovery stage goes to one or more fresh skeptics prompted to refute it. The orchestrator integrates only the claims that survive. Independent contexts are the point: a verifier that watched the discovery happen inherits its blind spots. A fresh one must reconstruct the evidence, and plausible-but-wrong findings die in the reconstruction. For expensive claims, several skeptics vote.

Loop until dry

For discovery jobs with unknown size (bug hunts, dependency audits), dispatch finder waves until two consecutive waves come back empty. A fixed worker count encodes a guess about how much there is to find; the loop replaces the guess with evidence.

What goes wrong

  • Workers collide. Two subagents editing the same file produce garbage merges. Either shard by disjoint scope or give each worker an isolated copy of the workspace and reconcile afterward.
  • You trust findings raw. A worker's confident summary is a claim, and claims from a context you cannot inspect deserve the judge treatment before they drive decisions.
  • The orchestrator micromanages. If the parent re-reads everything a worker read to check its work, the ledger collapses. You are back to one fat context that costs four times as much.
  • You orchestrate everything. A task that fits comfortably in one window with room to think is cheaper, simpler, and easier to debug as one agent. Orchestration pays off past a threshold of scale or required confidence; below that threshold it is pure overhead.

When to reach for it

Three signals point to orchestration, and any one of them is enough. The reading exceeds the window, as in audits and codebase-wide questions. The work shards into independent units whose results are easy to state compactly. Or the answer needs confidence that one perspective cannot provide, so you buy independent perspectives and let them disagree. If none of the three applies, stay in one context, which is cheaper and easier to debug.

Effort levels compose with all of this: the reasoning effort piece covers how one agent spends serial compute, and orchestration spends compute across agents. Mechanical shards run cheap and low; the judge that decides what ships runs high. The knobs are orthogonal, and mature setups turn both.
